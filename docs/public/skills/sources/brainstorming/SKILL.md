# 头脑风暴（Brainstorming）

改编自 obra/superpowers（MIT License），原作者 Jesse Vincent

## 触发条件

- **任何创造性工作之前都必须用**：做新功能、建组件、加功能、改行为、做规划——软件内还是软件外都算（一次演讲、一门生意、一次装修）。
- 对方说了一个想法，但你感觉他自己还没完全想清楚要什么、为什么要 → 用这个技能先聊清楚再动手。
- **不适用**：需求里"所有关键信息都在了"的快任务（比如"把图标改成矢车菊蓝"）——直接做，做完说一声就行。

## 核心思想

你很擅长建造。你会按自己"以为"对方想要的样子去建，而这个"以为"通常比你感觉的要薄得多。这个技能的作用，是在动手之前搞清楚对方**到底想要什么，以及为什么**。

人常常还没完全想清楚自己要什么。好的问题能帮他们把思路走完。当你理解了"为什么"，那些他们没提过的上百个小决定，你就能按他们会做的方式去做。

## 第一步：我到底知道多少？

回复之前先问自己：关于对方要什么、为什么要，我到底知道多少？

- **所有关键信息都在需求里**（"把图标改成矢车菊蓝"）：这是个又快又清楚的任务。直接做，说一声做了什么。
- **有关键的东西缺失**：开始对话。

## 你的第一句话

第一句话只干两件事，按这个顺序：

1. 一句话告诉对方：如果你不想回答问题、想让我直接开干，说一声就行。如果他真选了跳过，这个技能就结束了：把重大的"怎么做"选择（平台、语言、媒介）用一句话 plain 地说出来，然后按正常流程做他要的事。
2. 一个开放问题，让他开始描述。要问具体的时刻或画面："你上次希望有这东西，是在什么场景下？"、"到时候房间里会有哪些人？"

整条消息就这两样。

## 对话怎么进行

**每条消息以一个问题结尾**：一句话，不许后面挂一串示例答案。用大白话，语气比对方当前水平稍高一点。用他的词汇，永远不用流程黑话。

简洁，重要的先说。汇报发现时给最重要的几条，剩下的说"还有，要听吗"。

**让他描述**。开放问题永远先行。他自己的话里带着意图，是你的选项给不了的。只有当他卡住时，才给一个简短的菜单。他说"随便你"、"你觉得呢"时，就给一个具体的提议。

**怎么做（媒介与工具）**。每个项目都架在几个选择上：媒介、工具、材料、资源——软件就是平台、语言、库；艺术品就是媒介。早点搞清楚：哪些选择他想自己定，哪些交给你，哪些是他手头已有的。懂行或有主见的人会想聊透；指望你的人需要你给靠谱的默认选项，用大白话说出来。有现成工作，就沿用它已有的东西。你做的任何选择都记成"你的决定"，永远不要写成"他同意过的"。

**要覆盖的地盘**（这是地盘清单，不是提问脚本——**永远不要照着念**）：

- 这是个什么东西，特别在哪
- 为什么是现在，他眼中的成功是什么样
- 目标、非目标、反目标（做到什么样算失败，哪怕技术上跑通了）
- 见过什么先例：喜欢过什么、讨厌过什么
- 现状：现在有什么、试过什么
- 他在意的细节

**管用的问题**都锚定在具体的事上："带我走一遍你上次遇到这事的完整经过……"、"如果只能做好其中一件，你选哪个？"、"我要是哪块搞错了会让你皱眉？"。一个他能纠正的猜测（"我猜是因为 X 一直咬着你不放？"）常常比一个问题更管用。

**主动提议去"侦察"**。当地面信息够了、去看一眼会有帮助时，主动说：我可以去看你的代码库/项目、你自己的文件和数据，或者上网查。说清楚你想看什么。去不去他定。

**私下想得宽**。在提议任何东西之前，自己先想几个方案，把弱的毙掉——包括那些跟他说的"为什么"打架的。只有当对比能帮他做决定时，才把对比摆出来。

**能展示就别光说**。凡是"看了比读了更明白"的时刻，就用可视化（见下）：对方会看的东西（屏幕、页面、打印稿、招牌）；布局（屏幕、房间、日程）；流程或步骤序列；长得不一样的几个选项；看比读容易的结构（图、时间线、地图）。

**用 spike 探路**。Agent 的工作是瀑布流，但是极快的那种。一个一次性的快速试做，常常是搞清楚"他要什么"或"这事行不行"最便宜的办法。觉得有用就提议。他只想 spike 时别挡路。Spike 不写测试（或只写最少的），不做加固，不走计划-实现-评审流程。把 spike 学到的东西带回对话里。

## 随时复述（Play it back）

边聊边复述。一旦你把某一块理解到能讲清楚（比如"这是给谁用的、用来干嘛"，或"这一块怎么运作"），就用 200–300 字讲回去，然后回去问下一块。复述和提问整场交替；最后一块覆盖剩下的所有内容。

凡是你在猜的，标成"我的猜测"；只有他亲口说的，才记成"他的"。每段复述结尾问：哪里不对、缺了什么。

发出前检查一次：这段描述的是他会看的东西吗（页面、屏幕、日程、打印稿）？如果是，而他还没见过可视化提议，就先发提议（单独一条消息，见下），这段先压住。等他接受了，描述旁边配上 mockup。

## 定规模

搞清楚他要什么之后，选一个规模，用大白话说出来。他可以推翻。

| 规模 | 完整描述是 | 然后 |
|---|---|---|
| 又快又清楚的任务 | 需求本身 | 直接做 |
| 小改动 | 在聊天里讲清楚 | 按正常流程建 |
| 需要书面设计的项目 | 一份书面设计文档 | 出完整计划（软件：用"写计划"技能） |

如果做到一半变大了，停下来，说出来，升一档。

如果这其实是几个独立项目，说出来，定个顺序，一次头脑风暴一个。

## 书面设计文档

给项目写一份文档：一个懂行的建造者照着它能直接出计划，不用再回来问你的人。包含：

- 意图和为什么
- 目标、非目标、反目标
- 约束
- 他定下来的"怎么做"的部分，按他定的样子写
- 留给建造者的部分

写完后找个"建造者视角"检查一遍：把文档给一个没参与对话的人（或你自己扮演），看他会问什么问题。问题分成三堆：

- **对话里已有答案的**：把答案写进文档。
- **次要的**：靠谱的建造者自己能定、不影响建什么。你定，文档里标成"我的决定"。
- **只能他回答的**：而且答案会影响建什么。这一堆一次问完，最重要的先问。把他的回答更新进文档，然后交出去，附一份"你定的东西"清单让他核对。检查只产生这一轮问题。

## 铁门（HARD-GATE）：没批准，不许开工

**在对方批准完整描述之前，什么都不许建**：小改动批的是聊天里的描述，项目批的是文档。"建"包括写产品代码、搭架子、装依赖、建项目、调用实现类技能。**看和探索没问题**。

两个例外：如果他一开始就选了跳过提问，技能结束，门也跟着结束。他点头了的 spike 可以建，但永远标成一次性的；想把 spike 的产出留下来，那是**新的请求**，重新过这道门。

项目文档批准后，下一步是计划。软件项目调用"写计划"技能，不调用别的。

## 红线：这些念头一出现就停下

| 念头 | 现实 |
|---|---|
| "我给几个选项，省他力气" | 选项会引导人。先让他自己描述。 |
| "我填几个靠谱的默认值" | 可以，但要说出来。他没听过的默认，是他没机会拒绝的默认。 |
| "我先解释一下流程" | 直接问你的问题。流程自己会显现。 |
| "他说'听起来不错'" | 批准只覆盖你展示过的东西。没写出来的描述不算被批准。 |
| "spike 跑通了，我接着往下建" | 留下来就是新请求。回门里重新过。 |

## 可视化搭档

一个浏览器页签，用来放 mockup、图和原型。它是工具，不是模式：接受了它不代表每个问题都去浏览器里聊。

**只在需要时提议**。第一次出现"看了更明白"的时刻再提议，永远不要开场就提。提议单独一条消息，不掺别的：

> "这块可能看一眼更清楚，要我开个浏览器页签放几个 mockup 吗？"

他拒绝了就留在文字里，除非他再提，否则不提第二次。

**每个问题都问一句：他看了会更明白吗？** Mockup、布局、图、并排对比放浏览器；需求、范围、权衡、概念选择留在文字里。聊 UI 的话题不自动等于视觉问题。

## English Version

Adapted from obra/superpowers (MIT License) by Jesse Vincent.

### When to use

You MUST use this before any creative work — creating features, building components, adding functionality, modifying behavior, or planning anything new, in software or out of it (a talk, a business, a renovation). Use it when the request is missing something non-trivial and you need to find out what they actually want and why before anything gets built. Skip it only for quick, fully-specified tasks.

### Core idea

You are very good at building — you build what you believe your human partner wants, and that belief is usually thinner than it feels. This skill is for discovering what they actually want, and why, before anything gets built. People often haven't finished working out what they want; good questions help them think it through. When you understand the why, you make the hundred unmentioned decisions the way they would have.

### First: what do you actually know?

Before replying, ask: what do I know about what they want, and why? If everything that matters is in the request ("make the icon cornflower blue"), just do it and say what you did. If something non-trivial is missing, start the conversation.

### Your first message

Two things, in this order: (1) one line saying they can skip the questions and have you just start — if they take it, the skill is done: state any significant how-it-gets-made choice (platform, language, medium) in one plain line, then do the work normally; (2) one open question that gets them describing — anchor in a real moment or concrete picture ("What's the moment you find yourself wishing this existed?"). That's the whole message.

### The conversation

End each message with one question: a single sentence, no list of example answers. Plain language, pitched just above your partner. Match their vocabulary; never use process jargon. Lead with what matters.

**Get them describing.** Open questions first — their own words carry intent your options can't. Offer a short menu only when they're stuck. When they say "just guess", propose something concrete.

**How it gets made.** Every project rests on choices of medium, tools, materials, resources. Find out early which choices they want to make, which they're handing to you, and what they already have. Give sensible defaults in plain words to those counting on you. Build on existing work's choices. Your calls go in the design as your calls — never as things they agreed to.

**Ground to cover** (territory, not a script — never read aloud): what kind of thing this is and what's special; why now and what success looks like; goals, non-goals, anti-goals (what would make it a failure even if it technically works); prior art loved or hated; current state and what's been tried; details they care about.

**Questions that work** anchor in specifics: "Walk me through the last time…", "If it could only do one of these well, which?", "What would make you wince if I got it wrong?" A correctable guess ("I'm guessing this is because X keeps biting you?") often beats a question.

**Offer recon.** Once grounded and looking would help, offer to look — their codebase, their files, or the web. Say what you'd look for; they decide.

**Think wide privately.** Generate several ideas before proposing anything; drop weak ones, including any fighting their stated why. Show comparisons only when it helps them decide.

**Show, don't tell.** Offer the visual companion whenever seeing beats reading: things they'll look at, layouts, flows, visibly different options, structures easier seen than read (diagrams, timelines, maps).

**Spike to feel things out.** A quick throwaway build is often the cheapest way to learn what they want or whether something works. Offer one when useful; get out of the way when they just want to spike. Spikes get no (or minimal) tests, no hardening, skip plan/implement/review. Bring learnings back into the conversation.

### Play it back

As soon as you understand one part well enough to describe it, describe it back in ~200–300 words, then return to questions. Playback and questions alternate; mark guesses as guesses — only what they said counts as theirs. End each chunk asking what's wrong or missing. Before sending a chunk describing something visual (a page, screen, schedule), send the visual-companion offer first (its own message) and hold the chunk until accepted.

### Size the work

Once you understand, name a size in plain words (they can override): a quick clear task → the request itself → do it; a small change → in chat → build through the normal workflow; a project → a written design document → a full plan (for software: the writing-plans skill). If it grows mid-task, stop, say so, step up a size. If it's really several independent projects, say so, agree an order, brainstorm one at a time.

### The written design

Write a plain document a talented domain builder could plan from without returning to your partner: intent and why; goals, non-goals, anti-goals; constraints; the decided parts of the how, as decided; what's left to the builder. Then run a builder check (a fresh reviewer reads it); sort their questions into three piles — already answered (write into the doc), minor (settle yourself, mark as your call), theirs (only your partner can answer and it changes what's built). Bring the "theirs" pile in one message, most important first; update the doc; hand it over with your calls listed. That's the only question round the check produces.

### HARD-GATE

Nothing gets built until your partner approves the full description — in chat for a small change, the document for a project. Building includes product code, scaffolding, installing dependencies, creating projects, invoking implementation skills. Reading and exploring are fine. Two exceptions: they opted out of questions (skill done, gate gone); an approved spike may be built but stays labeled throwaway — keeping its output is a new request back through the gate. After a project's document is approved, the next step is the plan (for software: writing-plans, no other skill).

### Red flags

"I'll offer options to save them effort" → options steer; get them describing first. "I'll fill in sensible defaults" → fine, but say them aloud; an unheard default can't be rejected. "I should explain the process first" → ask your question; the process shows itself. "They said 'sounds good'" → approval covers only what you showed them. "The spike works, I'll keep building on it" → keeping it is a new request; back through the gate.

### Visual companion

A browser tab for mockups, diagrams, prototypes — a tool, not a mode. Offer just-in-time, the first time a seeing-beats-reading moment arises, as its own message: "This might be easier if I show you. Want me to open a browser tab with some mockups?" If declined, stay in text and don't offer again unless they raise it. Per question, ask: would they understand this better by seeing it? Mockups and layouts go in the browser; requirements, scope, tradeoffs, and conceptual choices stay in text.
